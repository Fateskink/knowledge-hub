# What Is RAG? Retrieval-Augmented Generation Explained

Large Language Models are impressive, but they have a fundamental problem: their knowledge is frozen at the time of training. Ask an LLM about something that happened last week, about your company's internal documentation, or about a niche technical topic, and it may produce an answer that is outdated, wrong, or entirely fabricated.

**Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)** solves this problem by giving the LLM access to external knowledge at the time it generates a response. Instead of relying solely on what it learned during training, a RAG system first retrieves relevant documents from a knowledge base, then provides those documents to the LLM as context for generating its answer.

<Callout type="info">
Think of RAG like an open-book exam. Without RAG, the LLM must answer from memory alone. With RAG, it can look up the relevant information before answering — leading to more accurate, up-to-date, and verifiable responses.
</Callout>

## Why RAG Matters

There are several reasons RAG has become one of the most widely adopted patterns in AI applications:

1. **Reduced hallucinations:** When the LLM generates answers based on retrieved documents rather than memory, it is far less likely to make things up.
2. **Current information:** RAG can access up-to-date documents, even if the LLM's training data is months or years old.
3. **Domain-specific knowledge:** You can connect the LLM to your company's private documents, product manuals, or internal wikis — knowledge it was never trained on.
4. **Verifiability:** RAG systems can cite their sources, letting users verify the accuracy of the response.
5. **Cost efficiency:** It is far cheaper to update a document database than to retrain or fine-tune an LLM.

## How RAG Works: The Complete Pipeline

A RAG system operates in two main phases: **indexing** (preparing your documents) and **retrieval + generation** (answering questions).

<Diagram chart="graph TD\n    subgraph Indexing Phase\n    A[Documents] --> B[Chunk Documents]\n    B --> C[Generate Embeddings]\n    C --> D[Store in Vector Database]\n    end\n    subgraph Query Phase\n    E[User Question] --> F[Generate Query Embedding]\n    F --> G[Search Vector Database]\n    G --> H[Retrieve Relevant Chunks]\n    H --> I[Build Prompt with Context]\n    I --> J[LLM Generates Answer]\n    end\n    D -.-> G" />

### Phase 1: Indexing

Before you can answer questions, you need to prepare your knowledge base.

**Step 1: Collect Documents.** Gather the documents you want the system to know about — PDFs, web pages, Markdown files, database records, API responses, or any other text source.

**Step 2: Chunk the Documents.** Long documents need to be split into smaller, manageable pieces called **chunks**. A chunk might be a paragraph, a section, or a fixed number of tokens (typically 200-1000 tokens).

<Callout type="tip">
Chunking strategy matters a lot. Chunks that are too small lose context; chunks that are too large dilute the relevant information. A common approach is to use overlapping chunks — each chunk shares some text with its neighbors to preserve context at the boundaries.
</Callout>

**Step 3: Generate Embeddings.** Each chunk is converted into a numerical vector (an embedding) using an embedding model. These vectors capture the semantic meaning of the text — chunks about similar topics end up with similar vectors.

**Step 4: Store in a Vector Database.** The embeddings are stored in a specialized database designed for fast similarity search. Popular vector databases include Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma, Qdrant, and pgvector (for PostgreSQL).

### Phase 2: Retrieval and Generation

When a user asks a question, the system retrieves relevant context and generates an answer.

**Step 1: Embed the Query.** The user's question is converted into an embedding vector using the same embedding model.

**Step 2: Similarity Search.** The query embedding is compared against all chunk embeddings in the vector database. The most similar chunks (typically the top 3-10) are retrieved.

**Step 3: Build the Prompt.** The retrieved chunks are inserted into the LLM's prompt as context, along with the user's original question.

**Step 4: Generate the Answer.** The LLM reads the provided context and generates a response grounded in that information.

## Understanding Embeddings

Embeddings are the foundation of RAG. They translate text into a mathematical space where meaning is represented by position.

<Diagram chart="graph LR\n    A[How do I reset my password?] --> B[Embedding Model]\n    B --> C[Vector: 0.12, -0.45, 0.78, ...]\n    D[I forgot my login credentials] --> B\n    B --> E[Vector: 0.11, -0.43, 0.80, ...]\n    F[Best pizza recipes] --> B\n    B --> G[Vector: -0.67, 0.22, -0.31, ...]" />

Notice that the first two sentences, while using completely different words, are semantically similar — they are both about account access problems. Their embedding vectors would be very close to each other. The pizza recipe sentence is about a completely different topic, so its vector would be far away.

This is the magic that makes RAG work: **semantic search** finds relevant documents based on meaning, not just keyword matching. A query about "resetting credentials" will match documents about "password recovery" even if those exact words never appear.

## Vector Databases: The Knowledge Store

A vector database is a specialized data store optimized for storing and searching high-dimensional vectors. Unlike traditional databases that search by exact match or keyword, vector databases perform **approximate nearest neighbor (ANN)** search to find the most similar vectors efficiently.

<Diagram chart="graph TD\n    A[Traditional Database] --> B[Exact Match: SELECT * WHERE name = 'John']\n    C[Vector Database] --> D[Similarity Search: Find vectors closest to query vector]\n    D --> E[Returns ranked results by similarity score]" />

### Why Not Just Use a Regular Database?

A traditional keyword search for "How do I fix a broken deployment pipeline?" might miss a document titled "Troubleshooting CI/CD Issues" because the exact words do not match. A vector database would find it because the semantic meaning is similar.

<Callout type="info">
Many organizations use a hybrid approach: combine vector similarity search with traditional keyword search (like BM25) and merge the results. This technique, called **hybrid search**, often outperforms either method alone.
</Callout>

## RAG in Practice: A Concrete Example

Imagine you are building a customer support chatbot for a software company. Here is how RAG would work:

1. **Index your knowledge base:** Chunk and embed all your product documentation, FAQs, troubleshooting guides, and release notes.

2. **Customer asks:** "How do I configure SSO with Okta?"

3. **Retrieve:** The system finds the 5 most relevant chunks from your documentation — the SSO setup guide, the Okta integration page, and a troubleshooting FAQ about SSO errors.

4. **Generate:** The LLM receives these chunks as context and generates a step-by-step answer specific to your product, citing the relevant documentation sections.

Without RAG, the LLM would either hallucinate configuration steps or give generic SSO advice that does not apply to your product.

## Advanced RAG Techniques

As RAG has matured, several advanced techniques have emerged.

### Multi-Query RAG

Instead of searching with just the user's question, generate multiple variations of the query to capture different aspects of the intent. This increases the chance of finding all relevant documents.

### Re-Ranking

After initial retrieval, use a more powerful (but slower) model to re-rank the results by relevance. The initial vector search is fast but approximate; a re-ranker improves precision.

### Contextual Compression

Retrieved chunks often contain irrelevant information alongside the useful parts. A compression step extracts only the portions relevant to the question before passing them to the LLM.

### Agentic RAG

Combine RAG with an AI agent that can decide when to search, what to search for, and whether the retrieved results are sufficient — or if it needs to reformulate the query and search again.

## Common RAG Pitfalls

<Callout type="warning">
RAG is not a silver bullet. These common pitfalls can undermine the quality of your RAG system.
</Callout>

- **Poor chunking:** If chunks are too large or split in the wrong places, retrieval quality suffers.
- **Irrelevant retrieval:** The top search results may not actually answer the question. Always evaluate retrieval quality separately from generation quality.
- **Missing context:** Sometimes the answer requires information spread across multiple documents. Ensure your retrieval strategy can surface all relevant pieces.
- **Stale data:** If your knowledge base is not kept up to date, RAG will serve outdated information — confidently.
- **Over-reliance on context:** The LLM might ignore its own knowledge and rely too heavily on retrieved (possibly irrelevant) context.

In many real-world applications, the best approach combines both: fine-tune the model for the desired tone and behavior, then use RAG to provide current, accurate knowledge.
</Callout>

RAG has become the standard pattern for building AI applications that need accurate, up-to-date, and domain-specific knowledge. Whether you are building a customer support bot, an internal knowledge assistant, or a research tool, understanding RAG is essential for creating AI systems that people can actually trust.
