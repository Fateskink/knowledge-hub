# Prompt Engineering Basics

A prompt is the text you send to an AI model to get a response. **Prompt engineering** is the skill of crafting that text to get the best possible output. It is not magic — it is a structured approach to communication that anyone can learn.

The difference between a mediocre AI response and an excellent one often comes down to how you write your prompt. This article covers the most important techniques, with practical examples you can use immediately.

<Callout type="info">
Prompt engineering is not about tricking the AI. It is about communicating clearly — providing the right context, structure, and instructions so the model understands exactly what you need.
</Callout>

## Why Prompt Engineering Matters

LLMs are general-purpose tools. The same model that writes poetry can also debug code, summarize legal documents, or plan a marketing campaign. But it needs guidance to know which "mode" to operate in.

Think of it like giving instructions to a very capable but very literal assistant. The clearer and more specific your instructions, the better the result.

## The Anatomy of a Good Prompt

Every effective prompt contains some combination of these elements:

1. **Role/Context:** Who should the AI act as? What is the situation?
2. **Task:** What exactly should it do?
3. **Format:** How should the output be structured?
4. **Constraints:** What should it avoid? What are the boundaries?
5. **Examples:** What does a good response look like?

<Callout type="tip">
You do not need all five elements in every prompt. Simple tasks may only need a clear task description. Complex tasks benefit from all five.
</Callout>

## Core Prompting Techniques

### 1. Zero-Shot Prompting

Zero-shot prompting means giving the model a task with no examples. You rely entirely on the model's pre-trained knowledge.

**Example:**
```
Classify the following movie review as positive, negative, or neutral:
"The cinematography was stunning but the plot felt predictable and rushed."
```

This works well for straightforward tasks where the model already understands the concept. Most everyday interactions with AI use zero-shot prompting.

**When to use it:** Simple, well-defined tasks like classification, summarization, translation, or answering factual questions.

### 2. Few-Shot Prompting

Few-shot prompting provides the model with a few examples of the desired input-output pattern before presenting the actual task. This dramatically improves performance on tasks where the format or style matters.

**Example:**
```
Convert the following product descriptions to concise bullet points:

Description: "Our premium leather wallet features RFID blocking technology,
eight card slots, and a slim profile that fits comfortably in any pocket."
Bullets:
- RFID blocking technology
- 8 card slots
- Slim profile design
- Premium leather

Description: "This stainless steel water bottle keeps drinks cold for 24 hours
and hot for 12 hours, with a leak-proof lid and a capacity of 750ml."
Bullets:
- 24hr cold / 12hr hot insulation
- Leak-proof lid
- 750ml capacity
- Stainless steel construction

Description: "The wireless noise-canceling headphones offer 30-hour battery life,
Bluetooth 5.3, and a foldable design with memory foam ear cushions."
Bullets:
```

The model sees the pattern and produces consistent output that matches the format and level of detail from the examples.

<Callout type="info">
Research shows that few-shot prompting can improve accuracy by 10-30% on many tasks compared to zero-shot. The quality of your examples matters more than the quantity — 2-3 well-chosen examples often outperform 10 mediocre ones.
</Callout>

### 3. Chain-of-Thought (CoT) Prompting

Chain-of-thought prompting asks the model to show its reasoning step by step before giving a final answer. This significantly improves performance on tasks that require logic, math, or multi-step reasoning.

**Without CoT:**
```
Q: A store has 45 apples. They sell 60% on Monday and half of the remainder
on Tuesday. How many are left?
A: 9
```

**With CoT:**
```
Q: A store has 45 apples. They sell 60% on Monday and half of the remainder
on Tuesday. How many are left?
A: Let me work through this step by step.
1. Start with 45 apples
2. Monday: sell 60% of 45 = 27 apples sold, leaving 45 - 27 = 18 apples
3. Tuesday: sell half of 18 = 9 apples sold, leaving 18 - 9 = 9 apples
The answer is 9 apples.
```

You can trigger chain-of-thought reasoning simply by adding "Let's think step by step" or "Explain your reasoning" to your prompt.

<Diagram chart="graph TD\n    A[Problem Statement] --> B[Step 1: Identify Given Information]\n    B --> C[Step 2: Apply First Operation]\n    C --> D[Step 3: Apply Second Operation]\n    D --> E[Step 4: State Final Answer]\n    E --> F[Verify Result]" />

<Callout type="warning">
Chain-of-thought prompting uses more tokens (and therefore more cost and time) because the model generates the reasoning steps. Use it when accuracy matters more than speed — for math, logic, complex analysis, and coding problems.
</Callout>

### 4. System Prompts

A system prompt sets the overall behavior, personality, and constraints for the AI before the user conversation begins. It acts as a persistent instruction set that shapes every response.

**Example system prompt:**
```
You are a senior Python developer and code reviewer. When reviewing code:
- Focus on readability, performance, and security
- Suggest specific improvements with code examples
- Rate the code quality on a scale of 1-10
- Use a professional but friendly tone
- If you spot a security vulnerability, highlight it with [SECURITY] prefix
```

System prompts are powerful because they establish consistent behavior across an entire conversation without repeating instructions in every message.

### 5. Role Prompting

Assigning the AI a specific role can dramatically change the quality and style of its responses. The model adjusts its vocabulary, depth, and perspective based on the role.

**Example:**
```
You are an experienced data scientist explaining concepts to a marketing team
that has no technical background. Use analogies from everyday life and avoid
jargon. If you must use a technical term, define it immediately.
```

## Advanced Tips and Patterns

### Be Specific About Format

Instead of "Summarize this article," try:
```
Summarize this article in exactly 3 bullet points. Each bullet should be
one sentence, start with a verb, and capture a key finding.
```

### Use Delimiters for Clarity

When your prompt includes data or text to process, use clear delimiters:
```
Translate the text between the triple backticks to French.
Maintain the original formatting.

\```
Welcome to our platform. Please review the terms of service
before creating your account.
\```
```

### Iterative Refinement

Prompt engineering is rarely a one-shot process. Start simple, evaluate the output, and refine.

<Diagram chart="graph LR\n    A[Write Initial Prompt] --> B[Review Output]\n    B --> C{Satisfactory?}\n    C -->|No| D[Identify Issues]\n    D --> E[Refine Prompt]\n    E --> B\n    C -->|Yes| F[Use or Save Prompt]" />

### Constrain the Output

Tell the model what NOT to do:
```
Answer the customer's question about our return policy.
- Do NOT make up information not in the policy document below
- Do NOT promise exceptions to the policy
- If unsure, say "I'd recommend contacting our support team"
```

### Ask the Model to Verify

For important tasks, add a verification step:
```
After generating your response, review it for:
1. Factual accuracy
2. Completeness (did you address all parts of the question?)
3. Clarity (would a non-expert understand this?)

If you find issues, revise before presenting the final answer.
```

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

<Callout type="warning">
These are the most frequent prompt engineering mistakes beginners make.
</Callout>

1. **Being too vague.** "Tell me about marketing" will get a generic essay. "Give me 5 low-budget marketing strategies for a new SaaS product targeting small businesses" gets actionable advice.

2. **Overloading a single prompt.** If you need the model to do 10 different things, break them into separate prompts. Complex, multi-part prompts increase the chance of the model missing or conflating requirements.

3. **Not providing context.** The model cannot read your mind. If your question depends on specific context (your industry, audience, constraints), include it.

4. **Ignoring the output format.** If you need JSON, a table, or bullet points, say so explicitly. Otherwise, the model will choose its own format.

5. **Not iterating.** Your first prompt is a draft. The best prompt engineers treat their prompts as code — they test, evaluate, and refine them.

## Putting It All Together

Here is an example that combines multiple techniques:

```
System: You are a senior financial analyst. Be precise with numbers
and cite your reasoning.

User: I'm evaluating whether to invest in Company X. Based on the
financial data below, analyze the company's health.

Use chain-of-thought reasoning. Structure your analysis as:
1. Revenue trends
2. Profitability analysis
3. Key risks
4. Overall recommendation (Buy/Hold/Sell)

[Financial data here]

Format your response with clear headings and include a summary
table at the end.
```

This prompt uses role assignment, explicit structure, chain-of-thought instruction, and format specification — giving the model everything it needs to produce a high-quality analysis.

<Callout type="tip">
The best way to improve at prompt engineering is practice. Try different techniques with the same task and compare the results. Keep a collection of prompts that work well for your common tasks.
</Callout>

Prompt engineering is a skill that improves with practice. Start with the techniques in this article, experiment with your own variations, and you will quickly find that AI becomes a far more powerful tool in your hands.
